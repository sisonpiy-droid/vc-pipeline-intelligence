1. Your 60-second answer

"Vikram is a first-time founder raising a Seed round for a B2B logistics SaaS. He has 50 VCs and 32 angels to chase and no time to watch the news for all of them. My system checks the public web for each investor's activity in the last 30 days, every Monday and Thursday. Gemini Flash reads the evidence and judges how relevant it is. Fixed code, not the AI, turns that into a 0 to 100 follow-up score and one recommended action. Only four columns are written back to Supabase, so his notes and contact history can never be touched. He opens the app and sees who to email first, why, with the source link and the exact quote that proves it."

The five sentences to remember

  1. Evidence first: no source link and verbatim quote, no recommendation.
  2. AI judges, code decides: Gemini gives relevance (0 to 100); the score and action are arithmetic in code.
  3. A refresh can only write four columns: enforced three ways (code schema, database role, tests).
  4. A failed search is never called "quiet": unknown and quiet are different states, shown differently.
  5. A run is data, not a request: a durable queue survives serverless time limits and crashes.

The core is: refresh, score, write four columns safely, show the result. 3. The workflow, step by step

Two things happen on a schedule: the refresh (collect, judge, score, store) and the daily report. The founder's own actions (reading, editing notes, sending mail from his own app) are separate and never mixed into the refresh.

1 TriggerCron Mon+Thu, or "Refresh now"
›
2 Queue1 run + 1 row per investor
›
3 SearchTavily, last 30 days
›
4 FetchOpen page, read its own date
5 ValidateInvestor named? in window? duplicate?
›

›
7 ScoreFixed rules, 0 to 100, action
›
8 WriteOne transaction, 4 columns only
  1. Trigger. Vercel Cron calls GET /api/cron/refresh with a secret (CRON_SECRET). Anyone without the secret gets 401. A manual "Refresh now" button exists, limited to one per 10 minutes to protect the free search credits.
  2. Plan the run. The planner decides which investors are worth searching: it skips anyone checked in the last 36 hours, searches quiet investors about weekly, and stops when the monthly credit budget (900) is reached. It creates a runs row and one run_items row per investor.
  3. Process in chunks. The function claims 3 investors at a time (FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED) until its 240-second budget ends, then hands the rest to a fresh invocation with an authenticated POST. Crash-safe: stuck items are reclaimed after 5 minutes.
  4. Research. One Tavily search per investor for the last 30 days (dated results only). Each result page is fetched safely (SSRF-guarded, robots.txt respected) and its publication date is read from the page itself (JSON-LD or Open Graph).
  5. Validate in code. Does the page name the investor? Is the date inside the window and consistent? Is it a real event rather than a profile or directory page? Is it a duplicate of another event? Each rejection stores a reason.
  6. Gemini Flash judges. One batched call per investor returns structured JSON: is the investor genuinely involved, what kind of signal, relevance 0 to 100 to a B2B logistics Seed company, confidence, a one-sentence summary and a quoted excerpt. The quote must appear verbatim in the page we fetched, otherwise the item is rejected.
  7. Score in code. Pure function over the stored signals, the investor's profile and relationship status: recency 30 + relevance 30 + evidence 20 + fit 15 + relationship 5, with a relevance gate. The action (reach out now, prepare outreach, wait, research, no action) follows from the score.
  8. Write safely. One database transaction per investor, as the restricted role vc_refresh: save evidence, coverage and an audit line, then update only the four columns. If anything fails, the transaction rolls back and the investor keeps its previous score.
  9. Finish honestly. A run is "succeeded" only when every investor finished; "partial" if some failed. The dashboard works out each investor's state when it reads: active, older signals, quiet (every source answered), needs review (partial), failed.
  10. Deliver. The web app shows the ranked queue and the weekly brief. A daily Telegram message (05:30 UTC = 11:00 IST) summarises who to email first. The founder opens a draft and sends it from his own mail app. The app never sends email.

6. Backend deep dive

The score (pure code, no model in the arithmetic)

PartPointsHow it is worked out
Recencyup to 30Full points for a signal up to 3 days old, then falling in a straight line to 20% at day 30; multiplied by the relevance gate
Relevanceup to 30Gemini's 0 to 100 judgement of fit to "B2B logistics / supply-chain SaaS", scaled to 30
Evidenceup to 20Confidence: high 1.0, medium 0.65, low 0.3; +0.15 if a second distinct relevant event exists; also gated
Fitup to 1540% sector, 40% stage, 20% geography (logistics and seed-stage India score highest; unknown scores lower)
Relationshipup to 5Not contacted 0.5, contacted 0.8, in conversation 1.0, passed or committed 0

The two rules that matter more than the weights. (1) Relevance gate: recency and evidence only count as far as the signal is relevant (relevance ÷ 70, capped at 1). (2) Relevance floor of 40: below it a signal is stored but earns nothing. Without these, a fresh, well-sourced but irrelevant article would score 72; with them it scores 18.

Worked example A

2 days old, relevance 90, high confidence, seed-stage logistics investor in India, not yet contacted:
30 + 27 + 20 + 15 + 2.5 = 94.5 → 95, "reach out now".

Worked example B

Same investor, but the article's relevance is 15:
0 + 0 + 0 + 15 + 2.5 = 18. No signal credit; the score cannot exceed fit plus relationship (20).

Actions (first match wins): passed or committed → no action · awaiting reply (≤ 14 days) → wait · relevant and ≥ 75 → reach out now · relevant and 55 to 74 → prepare targeted outreach · relevant and < 55 → research further · no relevant signal but partial coverage → research further ("absence is not conclusive") · otherwise no action. The 75/55 thresholds come from a score grid in docs/SCORING.md; the first version used 70/50 and let "adjacent" signals through.

The write boundary (three independent layers)

  1. Code: the only investor write is applySignalUpdate: a strict zod schema over exactly four fields; the SQL is built from a constant column list, never from the incoming object.
  2. Database: the refresh connects as role vc_refresh: UPDATE on those four columns only, no SELECT on personal_notes or contact_history, so even a careless select * fails.
  3. Tests: they snapshot the whole row before and after a refresh (success, repeats, partial failure, hostile model output) and assert it is identical outside the four columns, including updated_at.

Security in one paragraph

Constant-time secret comparison and fail-closed cron auth (no secret set means nobody is allowed). Input validation with zod. A fetcher that blocks internal addresses (SSRF), checks the address it actually connected to, re-checks every redirect and obeys robots.txt. Passcode lockout and a database cooldown on the manual trigger. No secrets in the browser or in logs. Row-level security on with no public policies, so Supabase's automatic API exposes nothing. Web text sent to the model is treated as untrusted. Known gap, stated openly: the passcode lockout counter lives in each server instance's memory.

Design decisions you can defend

  • Answer first. The home screen leads with "who to email this week", not a table of 82 rows.
  • Plain words. Labels like "Couldn't check yet" instead of jargon, and a "How we decided" explanation.
  • Honest states. Failed, partial and quiet look different, so the founder is never told "nothing happened" when a search failed.
  • Light and dark mode that follows the device and can be switched; responsive down to phone width (every page is tested at phone width); keyboard focus, a skip link and labelled controls; reduced-motion respected.
  • GSAP page choreography: titles rise in, rows and cards rise as they scroll into view; the page is fully visible without JavaScript.

10. Likely questions and how to answer them

Say it in your own words. Each answer is written to be spoken in 20 to 40 seconds.

Problem and design

Q1. Explain your project in one minute.
Use the 60-second answer on page 2. Finish with: "and the key promise is that the refresh can only write four columns, so the founder's notes are safe."
Q2. Why did you choose this workflow?
Because the work is slow and can fail partway: 82 investors, each needing a search, page fetches and an AI call. So I made a run into data in the database (a queue). That survives serverless time limits and crashes, gives an audit trail, and lets me resume. The steps themselves are fixed, so a plain pipeline is cheaper and easier to test than an agent.
Q3. Where exactly is the AI used, and where is it not?
AI reads and judges text: is the investor really involved, what kind of event, how relevant to logistics SaaS, a one-sentence summary, an opening line, and the chat. It does not choose dates, links or the score. Those come from page metadata, the search provider and code.
Q4. The brief says about 50 VCs. Why 82?
The workbook has 50 VC firms and 32 angels. I tracked all of them because angels matter at Seed. The demo accounts are labelled and never counted in the real numbers.
Q5. What is the single most important design decision?
Separating who owns what. The founder owns notes and contact history; the refresh owns four signal columns. I enforced that in the database with a restricted role, not just in code, because a rule the database enforces cannot be broken by a bug.

AI and scoring

Q6. How do you stop the AI hallucinating?
Four ways. Dates come from the page's own metadata, not the model. Links come from the search provider. The model must return structured JSON that is validated. And its quoted evidence must appear word for word in the page I fetched, otherwise the item is thrown away.
Q7. How is the score calculated?
Recency 30, relevance 30, evidence 20, fit 15, relationship 5. Gemini only supplies the relevance number; code does everything else. Recency and evidence are gated by relevance, and anything under relevance 40 earns nothing, so a fresh irrelevant article cannot score high.
Q8. Why those thresholds, 75 and 55?
I built a grid of scores by age and relevance for a perfect-fit investor. 75 needs a fresh, clearly relevant signal; a merely adjacent one (relevance about 55) lands in "prepare outreach" instead. My first version used 70 and 50 and let adjacent signals through, so I raised them. They are constants and should be recalibrated with real outcomes.
Q9. Why did you change the brief's weights? (You did not.)
I kept 30/30/20/15/5. What I added is structure: the relevance gate and the floor of 40. With the literal weights, a fresh, well-sourced, irrelevant article scored 72; with the gate it scores 18.
Q10. What if the web page tries to trick the model (prompt injection)?
Page text is treated as untrusted data, the model can only return a fixed JSON shape, it has no tools to call, and its quote must exist in the page. The chat fences web text as untrusted and answers only from the dashboard's own data.
Q11. How did you test the AI part?
Provider doubles for fast, repeatable tests (including hostile model output), plus 10 golden cases against live Gemini that all pass. The score itself is pure code, so it has ordinary unit tests.
Q12. The brief says Gemini Flash only. Why is there an OpenRouter backup?
It is the same Gemini Flash model, reached through a different door, and it is used only if Google fails, for example when the free quota runs out. The product never switches to a different model. It is optional, logged, and shown in the Integrations panel; removing the key turns it off. If you would rather see strictly Google-only, I can remove the key and the behaviour reverts to a clear error.

Backend and infrastructure

Q13. How do you handle 82 investors with a 300-second limit?
A run is rows in the database. Each invocation claims 3 investors at a time with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED until 240 seconds, then hands off to a fresh invocation. If a function dies, items are reclaimed after 5 minutes; if the hand-off is lost, the next trigger resumes the same run.
Q14. What does "quiet" mean, and how is it different from "failed"?
Quiet means every source answered and found nothing relevant in 30 days. Failed or partial means a search did not complete, so the activity is unknown. A failed item writes nothing and keeps its previous score, and the screen says "couldn't check yet", never "quiet".
Q15. How do you keep search costs inside the free tier?
A planner skips investors checked in the last 36 hours, searches quiet investors about weekly, and enforces a monthly credit budget of 900. The Runs page shows the credits used so far.
Q16. Why Monday and Thursday?
Vercel's free plan allows at most one cron a day, with up to about an hour of jitter. Monday and Thursday give even three- and four-day gaps and keep about 710 searches a month under the free allowance. The schedule is in vercel.json and a test keeps the dashboard's "next run" in step.
Q17. What happens if two refreshes start at once?
A partial unique index in the database allows only one active run, so one wins and the other is told "already running". Overlap protection is the database's job, not application logic.
Q18. Why Next.js for both the UI and the API?
One deployment and one language. Server components read the database directly, so no database client or secret reaches the browser, and the API routes sit next to the pages that use them.
Q19. Why Postgres directly and not the Supabase client library?
One secret instead of several, direct use of roles and grants (which the write boundary needs), and the same SQL runs in PGlite in the tests. Row-level security is still on with no public policies, so Supabase's automatic API exposes nothing.

Data safety and privacy

Q20. How do you guarantee the notes are never overwritten?
Three layers: a strict four-field schema in code, a database role that cannot read or write those columns, and tests that compare the whole row before and after. I also verified the role against the live database.
Q21. Is the website secure? Anyone can open it.
Yes, it is an open demo by choice so examiners can try it. Secrets stay on the server. Machine routes need a secret and fail closed. Manual refresh has a cooldown, and the chat is rate limited and cannot see notes or contacts. A real deployment would add sign-in; I say so in the docs.
Q22. You hold thousands of private contacts. Is that shown publicly?
No. They sit in a locked table with row-level security and no grants. The app only counts them and offers a search that lists organisations, never a person's name, email, phone or profile. A test fails if any personal field ever appears in a result, and another checks that nothing in the app writes to that table.
Q23. What is SSRF and how do you prevent it?
It is tricking a server into fetching internal addresses. My fetcher blocks private and internal addresses, validates the address it actually connected to (so DNS tricks fail), re-checks each redirect, and respects robots.txt.
Q24. Does the app send emails?
Never. It prepares a draft with your pitch and Calendly link and opens it in your own mail app. Moving a card to Contacted is a founder action with one-click undo, because opening a draft is not proof it was sent.

Frontend

Q25. Why did you design the home screen this way?
The founder wants an answer, not a spreadsheet. So it leads with the investors to email this week, each with its evidence, then the counts, then the rest. Everything else is one click away.
Q26. What did you do for accessibility and responsiveness?
A skip link, labelled controls, visible keyboard focus, readable contrast in both light and dark themes, reduced-motion support, and browser tests at phone widths that fail on horizontal scrolling or console errors.
Q27. Where is GSAP used, and does the page work without it?
For the entrance of titles, the cards, and rows that rise as you scroll. Content is only hidden by the script, never by the stylesheet, so the page is fully visible without JavaScript; with reduced motion nothing animates.

Limits and the future

Q28. What is the weakest part?
Recall. One search per investor and no crawl of every firm's site, so I can miss news. I mitigate it by being honest: coverage gaps are shown and "no signal" is only claimed when every source answered. Next step: per-firm feeds or sitemaps with snapshot diffing.
Q29. How would it scale to 5,000 investors?
The queue already scales horizontally, since work is split into small claims. Cost grows linearly with searches and AI calls, so I would move to paid search and AI tiers, batch by priority, and search quiet investors less often.
Q30. What would you build next?
Reply tracking so the app learns from outcomes, recalibrating the weights from real results, deeper per-firm crawling, and sign-in with Supabase Auth for a real deployment.
Q31. What is not finished or not verified?
Be direct: "The first scheduled run that actually searches is today at 09:30 IST; earlier runs prove the pipeline end to end. Telegram is connected and tested with a live message. The weights are reasoned, not yet calibrated on outcomes."
Q32. Did you consider an MCP server or an agent framework?
Yes, and left them out on purpose. The brief needs a scheduled job and a database, not an agent interface. An MCP server would be a second way into the data, which weakens the main guarantee. A fixed pipeline is easier to test and audit than an agent.
Q33. If Gemini is down at 09:30 on a cron day, what does the founder see?
The system tries the backup route. If that is also down, those investors fail, keep their previous scores, and are labelled "couldn't check yet". The run ends "partial", the Runs page shows why, and the next trigger retries. Nothing wrong is written.
Q34. How do you know a score is right?
Every score shows its five components, the lead signal, the source and the quote, so a person can check it in seconds. The thresholds were chosen from an inspectable grid, and the what-if page shows how changing weights moves the ranking.
Q35. What is the difference between "fit" and "relevance"?
Relevance is about the signal: how much a piece of news matters to a B2B logistics Seed company. Fit is about the investor: sector, stage and geography. Both are kept apart in the breakdown, along with relationship readiness and evidence confidence.

12. Plain-words glossary

TermMeaning
CronA timer that calls a URL on a schedule. Here: Monday and Thursday for the refresh, daily for the report.
Serverless functionCode that runs only when called and is stopped after a time limit (300 s here). That is why a run is split into chunks.
Durable queueThe to-do list stored in the database, so work survives crashes and restarts.
Row-level security (RLS)A Postgres switch that denies access to rows unless a policy allows it. Here it is on with no public policy.
Database role / grantA login identity and the exact rights it has. The refresh's role can update four columns and nothing else.
zodA library that checks data has exactly the expected shape, used on AI output and API input.
SSRFTricking a server into fetching internal addresses; blocked by the safe fetcher.
IdempotentDoing it twice has the same result as once. Re-running an import or a refresh does not create duplicates.
HallucinationWhen a model states something untrue. Countered by verifying dates, links and quotes in code.
Structured outputForcing the model to answer in a fixed JSON shape instead of free text.
PGliteA tiny Postgres that runs inside the tests, so tests use the real migrations.
SignalOne dated, sourced piece of evidence about an investor (a fund close, a new investment, an interview).
